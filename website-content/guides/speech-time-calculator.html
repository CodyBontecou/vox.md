The short answer

At a planning pace of 130–150 words per minute, 1,000 words take about 7 to 8 minutes (6:40 to 7:42), and a 5-minute speech needs about 650–750 words. Those two paces are this page’s presets, not a measured average. Rehearse once and measure your own pace for a real number.

The calculator below has three modes: script to time, time to words, and measure your real pace from a rehearsal transcript. The word counter reads Markdown the way a listener hears it, so headings, bullets, link addresses, [[wikilinks]] and frontmatter pasted from Obsidian do not inflate the count. It runs entirely in this page: your text is never uploaded, and the page makes no network requests.

3. Typical speaking rates, with sources

There is no single correct speaking rate, and a figure without a source is only a guess. These are the rates we could confirm in the sources themselves. Each describes a particular setting, so none of them is a target for your talk.

SettingRate stated by the sourceSource
Telephone conversations between strangers on assigned topics (2,438 English Switchboard conversations): all words from both speakers divided by total elapsed time196 WPM on average; individual conversations ranged from 111 to 291 WPMYuan, Liberman & Cieri (2006), Interspeech
The same conversations, excluding silences and non-speech noise236 WPM on average; conversations ranged from 158 to 312 WPMYuan, Liberman & Cieri (2006)
Adults reading aloud, a meta-analysis of 77 studies with 5,965 participants183 WPM on average (95% confidence interval 177–189)Brysbaert (2019), Journal of Memory and Language
Adults reading silently in English, a meta-analysis of 190 studies with 18,573 participants238 WPM for non-fiction and 260 WPM for fiction, on averageBrysbaert (2019)

What these figures do and do not tell you:

  • They are higher than this page’s presets. The 130 and 150 WPM presets are a planning rule for a talk with pauses and slide changes, which is our rule of thumb and not a finding. If you read your script aloud without stopping, expect something closer to the reading-aloud figure.
  • Counting method changes the number. Yuan et al. get 196 WPM when they divide by all elapsed time and 236 WPM when they remove silences, from the same conversations. Your recording’s length includes your pauses, so the pace you measure in mode 3 does too.
  • Pace varies by language, speaker and setting. Yuan et al. report that speaking rate changes with turn length, topic, whether the speakers know each other and the speaker’s age. Brysbaert reports lower reading rates for children, older adults and readers of English as a second language, and that rates in other languages can be predicted from how many words each language needs to convey the same message as English. Word counts are not comparable across languages.
  • Reading aloud is not giving a talk. The studies behind the reading-aloud figure are reading studies, not presentations. We did not find a primary source that sets a target pace for a presentation, so none is given here.

4. Why real talks run longer than words ÷ WPM

Dividing words by a pace gives the time you spend speaking. A real talk also has time you spend not speaking: a breath between paragraphs, a beat while the slide changes, a laugh or a question from the audience, a sip of water. The calculator lets you add these on top.

  • Pause per paragraph. Seconds added for every blank-line separated block in a pasted script. A pause of 2 to 3 seconds is a reasonable place to start for a spoken talk.
  • Slides and pause per slide. Seconds added for each slide change, for the moment it takes the audience to look at the new slide. Try 3 to 5 seconds, longer for a slide you want people to read.
  • Time kept for pauses (time to words). One number in seconds that is taken off the target length before the words are worked out.

These are planning rules of thumb from us, not statistics, and the right values depend on your style. A script of 1,000 words with 12 paragraphs and 10 slides, at 130 WPM with 3 seconds per paragraph and 4 per slide, is 7:42 of speech plus 36 seconds for paragraphs and 40 for slides (1:16 of pauses): 8:58 in total. The honest check is to rehearse once and time it, as below.

5. Measure your pace from a rehearsal

The honest way to estimate a talk is to rehearse it once, transcribe it and measure, rather than trust a generic 130 WPM. Your own pace can be well above or below any preset, and it changes with how well you know the material.

  1. Record a rehearsal. Speak the talk the way you will give it, pauses included. On iPhone or iPad, Apple Support’s Make a recording in Voice Memos explains how. On a Mac, see Create a recording in Voice Memos on Mac. Note how long the recording ran: that length comes from your recorder.
  2. Get a transcript. Voice Memos can show a transcript where it is available: see Apple Support’s View a transcription on iPhone, and our guide to transcribing Voice Memos on iPhone. You can also transcribe the recording with Vox.md, on the device. For a Zoom or YouTube recording with captions, the SRT and VTT to text converter turns the caption file into text.
  3. Paste and measure. Paste the transcript into mode 3 and enter the recording length. It divides the words in the transcript by the minutes, shows your measured WPM and how it compares with the presets, and works out the speaking time of your script at your pace.

Check the transcript against what you actually said before you trust the number. Speech recognition makes mistakes, and a missed or added word shifts the count. Rehearse the whole talk, trim the dead air at the start and end of the recording if there is a lot of it, and measure again after you edit the script.

Pauses are already inside the recording, so the script time in mode 3 does not add any. If your rehearsal had no slides or audience, allow a little extra for the real thing.

7. Questions

How many words is a 3, 5 or 10-minute speech?

At 130–150 WPM, a 3-minute speech is about 390–450 words, a 5-minute speech about 650–750 words and a 10-minute speech about 1,300–1,500 words. Across the slower and faster presets, 100–170 WPM, 5 minutes is 500–850 words. For comparison, the 183 WPM average that Brysbaert (2019) found for reading aloud would be about 915 words in 5 minutes. Add pauses, and rehearse to find your own number.

How long is a 1,000-word speech?

At 130 WPM, 7:42, and at 150 WPM, 6:40, before any pauses. At 100 WPM it is 10:00 and at 170 WPM it is 5:53. At the 183 WPM reading-aloud average from Brysbaert (2019), it is 5:28. With 12 paragraphs and 10 slides, add the pauses from the section above.

What is a good speaking pace for a presentation?

We did not find a primary source that sets a target pace for presentations. The sourced figures describe other settings: Yuan, Liberman and Cieri (2006) report 196 WPM across the elapsed time of telephone conversations, and Brysbaert (2019) reports 183 WPM for reading aloud. A slower planning pace such as 130–150 WPM leaves room for pauses and slide changes, but the reliable answer is your own pace from a rehearsal, as in measure your pace.

Does reading aloud take longer than reading silently?

Yes, on average. Brysbaert (2019) estimates 183 WPM for reading aloud and 238 WPM for silent reading of English non-fiction (260 WPM for fiction). At those averages the same non-fiction text takes about 30% longer aloud (238 ÷ 183 ≈ 1.3). Individual readers vary, so measure yourself.

Does the calculator upload my script?

No. The words are counted by a script in this page, on your device, and the page makes no network requests. It does not save your text: closing or reloading the page clears it.
